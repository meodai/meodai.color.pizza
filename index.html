I like exploring ideas by making things. I move between code, art and design, often starting with an obsession or a wild theory. What makes a palette beautiful? Can an algorithm encode beauty or certain aesthetic principles? What would an interface felt like a toy where exploration felt playful rather than just easy to use?

These questions tend to turn into prototypes, creative tools and open-source projects. Color has become a recurring topic, but the underlying interest is broader: finding unexpected connections between systems, aesthetics and human interaction, then making them tangible.

I work with teams and individuals to turn complex ideas into thoughtful, playful and useful digital experiences. And when an idea is too niche, too weird or simply too fun not to pursue, I build it myself.

Some projects I'm particularly proud of

Color Names

It started because Wikipedia's color names weren't nearly enough for me. Different reds shared the same name, and some of the existing names had histories I wasn't comfortable perpetuating. So I started collecting better ones. Almost 32,000 names later, it's become an ongoing personal project, a playground for data visualization, and an excuse to invent names wherever I travel. Many of them have stories attached.

Poline

I stumbled across a simple idea: draw a straight line through a polar representation of color and see what happens. The resulting palettes were surprisingly beautiful. Poline grew out of that discovery into a tool for exploring color relationships in a different way. It's a good example of what I love most: a small mathematical idea opening up a whole new creative space.

RYBitten

I've always been fascinated by the way people have tried to organise and explain color. RYBitten lets me explore and recreate color wheels from historical color books, bringing old systems into a new digital context. It's part research project, part generative playground, and an ongoing excuse to dig into the history of color.

heerich.js

I love Erwin Heerich's work, particularly its clarity, geometry and restrained use of form. It also connects to my longstanding love of pixel art and minimal 3D. I built a tiny engine that translates some of these ideas into plain SVG, so I can keep exploring them in my own work.

Charcuterie

I grew up with MS-DOS interfaces and the weird, wonderful typography in cracking groups' readme files. Unicode characters have been my icons ever since. Finding the right ones was always a pain, so I built my own tool. Naturally, that became an opportunity to experiment with embeddings and see how new ways of organising information can make searching more intuitive.

OKpalette

For years, I searched for the perfect library to extract dominant colors from images. Everything I tried had shortcomings, so I kept experimenting until I had something I was happy with. OKpalette is the result: a tool born out of frustration, curiosity and the conviction that sometimes the best solution is to build your own.

Ray Color

When we moved into our flat, I installed Philips Hue lights and noticed a tennis ball on the floor being lit from different directions. I started wondering what would happen if I sampled its colors under different lighting conditions. That little observation became Ray Color, a tool for generating palettes by changing the conditions around an object rather than manually choosing individual colors. I love the idea of treating a palette as a system that emerges, rather than a collection of isolated choices.

A bit about me

I grew up bilingual in Fribourg, obsessed with Lego and pretty much anything nerdy. My parents were against TV and video game consoles, so I'm forever grateful to my godfather for getting me the Game Boy I wasn't allowed to have, and to my uncles, who owned a suspiciously large number of consoles. And apologies to all the parents in Fribourg whose doorbells I rang at 7 in the morning to ask if their kids could play NES with me.

As a teenager, I started Frilan, organising a big LAN party in Fribourg. Later came graffiti, DJing, and a record collection that's still far too large. Looking back, they all had something in common: making things, experimenting, finding a scene, and sharing something with other people.

For a long time, I couldn't decide between art school and an apprenticeship as an Informatiker. I chose the latter, but never let go of the creative side. For years, I bounced between the two, until I realised I didn't have to choose. I could make a living doing both.

That turned out to be my favourite kind of life hack: getting paid to be curious about the things I love. Most days, it barely feels like work.

Today, I run Elastiq in Zürich and look after the z43 co-working space. I love sharing what I learn, whether that's speaking at conferences and universities or teaching creative programming to beginners, for free. Through Elastiq, I work on the intersection of design and technology, helping turn complex ideas into clear, engaging experiences. My own projects give me the freedom to follow ideas wherever they lead, learn by making, and share what I discover with others.

I rarely consider a project finished. I release things when they're good enough to be useful, then keep refining, expanding and occasionally breaking them for years. Open source is a big part of that: I like making tools that other people can use, learn from, adapt, or take somewhere I never imagined.

Along the way, I've had the chance to work with artists I admire, including Rik Oostenbroek on Verloop — Dégradé, a book exploring color and gradients. In 2026, I was genuinely surprised to win a Swiss Design Award in the Interaction Design category for my work around color.

I still mostly follow my curiosity, make things I wish existed, and share them in the hope that someone else finds them useful, inspiring, or simply fun to play with.
