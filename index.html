I work in the triangle between code, art and design, with a particular obsession for color: using algorithms to generate palettes that are both beautiful and useful. I love building interfaces where playful human interaction meets code, making complex systems tangible and fun to explore. Most of my work starts with curiosity, becomes a prototype, and often ends up as an open-source tool.

Color, generative art, interfaces — that's where I feel at home.

Some projects I'm particularly proud of

Color Names

It started at a former job: I wanted to explore colors, but Wikipedia's names weren't nearly enough, so different reds got the same name, and many were colonial, sexist or worse. Over the years it became my escape project. Whenever I travel I come up with new names, and with almost 32,000 of them, it's my playground for data visualization.

Poline

I discovered that drawing a straight line across a polar representation of color gives surprisingly beautiful palettes. Poline grew out of that one line between anchor colors.

RYBitten

A way to capture the color wheels of many historic color books. It's still my playground for recreating illustrations from old books about color.

heerich.js

I love Erwin Heerich's work, and it ties into my love for pixel art. Clear lines and minimal 3D are a playground I keep bringing into my designs, Charcuterie for example, so I built a tiny engine that draws them as plain SVG.

Charcuterie

I grew up on MS-DOS, loving its text interfaces and the art in cracking groups' readmes, and I still use Unicode characters as icons. Searching for them was painful, so I built my own way to find them, which also let me explore different embeddings and understand what they're good for.

OKpalette

I wanted palettes that catch the small, colorful details you remember an image for, not just its dominant colors.

Ray Color

A Philips Hue setup and a tennis ball on my table: every time the lights changed, the ball became a new palette, and the colors always fit together because they were made of the same light. Ray Color simulates that room.

A bit about me

I grew up bilingual in Fribourg, obsessed with Lego and pretty much anything nerdy. My parents were against TV and video game consoles, so I'm forever grateful to my godfather for getting me the Game Boy I wasn't allowed to have, and to my uncles, who happened to own a lot of consoles. And apologies to all the parents in Fribourg whose doorbells I rang at 7 in the morning, asking if their kids could play NES with me.

As a teenager, I started Frilan, organising a big LAN party in Fribourg. Later came graffiti, DJing, and a record collection that's still far too large.

For a long time, I couldn't decide between art school and an apprenticeship as an Informatiker. I went with the latter, but never let go of the creative side. For years, I bounced between the two, until I realised I didn't actually have to choose. I could make a living doing both.

Honestly, that's still one of my favourite things about my life: getting paid to be curious about the things I love. Most days, it barely feels like work.

Today, I run Elastiq in Zürich, look after the z43 co-working space, and teach creative programming to beginners, for free. I'm particularly drawn to generative art and to the space where code becomes a creative medium. I tend to release projects once they feel good enough, then keep tinkering with them for years. Nothing ever feels entirely finished, and I quite like it that way.

Along the way, I've had the chance to collaborate with people whose work I admire, including Rik Oostenbroek on Verloop — Dégradé, a book exploring color and gradients. In 2026, I was genuinely surprised to win a Swiss Design Award in the Interaction Design category for my work around color.

I still mostly follow my curiosity, make things that I wish existed, and share them in the hope that someone else finds them useful, inspiring, or just fun to play with.
